Every few months someone declares remote work dead, and every few months the listings disagree. Because we aggregate job boards with very different cultures — remote-first boards, European boards heavy on hybrid roles, general boards with everything — our dataset gives an unusually wide-angle view of where tech work actually happens. This guide is about what the listings say, and how to use it when the work model is a hard constraint for you rather than a preference.

The three-way split, honestly measured

Our pipeline classifies every listing as Remote, Hybrid or On-site by reading the title, the location field and the description together — because those three routinely contradict each other. A title says "Remote", the description says "2 days per week in our Berlin office". In our classification, hybrid beats remote whenever both signals appear, because the office days are the constraint that actually shapes your life. The company calling it "remote-friendly" doesn't move your apartment closer to Berlin.

One honest caveat about any aggregated remote number, including ours on the insights page: the source mix matters. Several boards we index specialize in remote work, so our remote share runs higher than the overall market's. That makes the direction of the number trustworthy — rising or falling over months — while the absolute percentage should be read as "of the boards where tech people actually search", not "of all jobs on Earth". Any site that gives you one clean number without that caveat is selling something.

"Remote" has fine print. Read it.

The word "remote" in a listing spans at least four different contracts:

  • Remote, anywhere. Rare and getting rarer. Usually async-first companies with payroll infrastructure for many countries, or contractor arrangements.
  • Remote within a region. The most common flavor: "Remote — Europe", "Remote (US timezones)". This is a legal and timezone constraint, not a culture signal. Check the location field on our listings — we preserve exactly this qualifier.
  • Remote for now. Watch for "initially remote" or an office address lovingly described in a "remote" listing. Policy changes arrive with quarterly planning.
  • Remote, but with a gravity well. "Remote OK, but we prefer candidates near London." Translation: promotion decisions happen at lunch.

When we detect the hybrid patterns — explicit "hybrid", or the arithmetic kind, "3 days in the office" — we label the listing accordingly, so filtering by Remote on our job board already excludes the gravity wells that self-identify. The ones that don't self-identify are an interview question: "Walk me through a normal week for someone on this team who lives two timezones away."

The salary geometry of remote work

Remote listings disclose salaries noticeably more often than on-site ones — remote-first companies compete across borders, and a number in the listing is their cheapest recruiting advantage. But remote salaries also come with location-adjustment policies that a listing rarely spells out. The range you see may be the San Francisco band, the Lisbon band, or a single global band, and the difference between those can be a factor of two. If the listing shows a range and the company hires internationally, asking "is this range location-adjusted, and to where?" is the single highest-value clarifying question you can send before investing in the process.

Hybrid is a schedule, not a compromise

Hybrid roles get evaluated lazily — by candidates and employers both — as "remote minus" or "office lite". In listings, the tell for well-run hybrid is specificity: which days, decided by whom, for what activities. "Tuesdays and Wednesdays together for planning and reviews" is a team that thought about it. "Flexible hybrid model" usually means the policy is whatever your manager's manager last announced. On-site days are also commute costs, and commute costs are salary: a five-figure annual difference hides in "3 days per week" for anyone who doesn't live near the office. Do that arithmetic before comparing two offers whose numbers look similar.

Practical filtering strategy

  1. If remote is a hard constraint, filter for it structurally (our Remote filter uses the detected work model), then verify in the first call. Classification reads the listing; only a human can read the policy.
  2. Treat region-restricted remote as its own market. "Remote — Europe" roles compete for a different candidate pool than "Remote — anywhere", and salaries track the pool.
  3. For hybrid roles, convert office days into commute hours and money before comparing offers.
  4. Recheck the market quarterly. Work-model mix shifts faster than most job-search advice does — the live numbers are recalculated continuously precisely because last year's blog posts age badly.

The meta-lesson from watching thousands of listings: work model is where companies say the most about themselves in the fewest words. A listing that's precise about where and how you'll work tends to belong to a company that's precise about everything else, too.
